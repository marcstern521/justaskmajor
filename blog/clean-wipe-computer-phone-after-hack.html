How to clean or wipe a computer and phone after a hack.

By Marc Stern · 13 min read

One of the most common questions I get after someone has been hacked or scammed is, “Is my computer still safe to use?” It’s a fair question, and the honest answer is: it depends on what happened. If a password was stolen through a fake login page, the computer may be perfectly fine and the fix is to change passwords. If a stranger had remote control of your computer, or you opened a malicious attachment or installed something you shouldn’t have, the computer itself may be compromised, and changing passwords on it won’t help until the machine is cleaned.

This post walks through how to decide how much cleanup you need, and then how to do it, from a basic scan, to removing remote-access tools and suspicious browser add-ons, to a full wipe and reinstall that gets you a clean slate. It covers Windows computers, Macs, iPhones, and Android phones, and it’s written so that a non-technical person can follow along, ideally with a family member sitting next to them.

This is part of a series. The starting point is Hacked? What to Do in the First Hour, which covers securing accounts. A companion post covers common scams and fake support calls, and another covers phone and home Wi-Fi security.

Do this first, from a different device: If you think a device is compromised, change your most important passwords (email, bank, Apple ID or Google account) from a different, trusted device, such as a family member’s phone or a computer you know is clean. Changing them on the infected machine can hand the new passwords right back to whoever is watching.

First, decide how much cleanup you need

Not every problem calls for wiping a computer. Here is a simple way to decide.

What happened What it probably means Level of cleanup
You entered a password on a fake login page, but nothing was installed The account was compromised, but the device is probably fine Change passwords, turn on MFA, run a scan to be safe
You clicked a suspicious link but nothing seemed to happen Possibly nothing, possibly a download Scan, check browser extensions, update everything
You opened a suspicious attachment or ran a downloaded file Malware may be installed Full scan with a second opinion, then consider a reset
You let a “support” person control your computer, or installed software at their request The computer should be treated as compromised Disconnect, back up your files carefully, wipe and reinstall
Your computer is behaving strangely (slow, pop-ups, new toolbars, programs you didn’t install) Possible malware or unwanted software Scan, remove unwanted apps, reset the browser; wipe if it persists
A phone is acting strangely, or you installed an app from outside the official store The phone may be compromised Remove the app, update, change passwords, factory reset if it persists

When in doubt, and especially when someone had remote access, I lean toward the full wipe. It takes an afternoon, it’s reliable, and it ends the guessing. A clean reinstall is the only way to be certain a computer is clean.

Before you do anything: protect what matters

Disconnect

If you think a computer is being actively controlled by someone, disconnect it from the internet now. Turn off Wi-Fi or unplug the network cable. This cuts off the remote connection.

Make a list of what you need to keep

Before wiping anything, figure out what’s irreplaceable: photos, documents, tax records, financial files, and anything else you can’t get back. Most other things (programs, settings) can be reinstalled.

Back up carefully (this is where people go wrong)

The risk with backing up an infected computer is copying the infection along with your files. A few rules keep this safe:

  • Copy only your personal files: documents, photos, videos, and similar data. Do not back up programs or the whole system image, because that would copy the problem.
  • Avoid copying executable files (.exe, .msi, .bat, .scr, .js, .vbs) and macros in documents you don’t recognize.
  • Use an external drive or a cloud service you can disconnect afterward. Don’t leave the drive plugged in during the wipe.
  • Scan the backup before you restore it onto the clean computer, using a trusted security tool.
  • If a cloud service like OneDrive, iCloud, or Google Drive has been syncing your files, check that your files are already safely in the cloud. Be aware that a compromised device can sync damage to the cloud too, so check version history if something looks wrong.

Write down what you’ll need after the wipe

After a reset you’ll need your Wi-Fi password, your Microsoft, Apple, or Google account sign-in (and MFA device), and the list of programs you use. Take a minute to jot these down on paper.

Sign out of things you won’t need again

If you’re getting rid of or selling a device, sign out of your accounts, turn off Find My or Find My Device, and remove it from your Apple ID or Google account. For a device you’re keeping, this matters less, but it helps the reset go smoothly.

Step 1: Try a thorough scan and cleanup (Windows)

If the situation doesn’t call for a full wipe, start here.

1. Update Windows. Open Settings, go to Windows Update, and install everything.

2. Run a full scan with Windows Security. Open Windows Security, choose Virus & threat protection, then Scan options, and select Full scan. Let it finish. Then run a Microsoft Defender Offline scan from the same screen. It restarts the computer and scans before Windows loads, which catches threats that hide.

3. Get a second opinion. A free scan from a reputable second tool, such as Malwarebytes, can catch things the first one missed. Download it only from the official website. Be wary of “free cleaners” that show up in ads or pop-ups, because many are scams.

4. Remove unwanted programs. Go to Settings, then Apps, then Installed apps. Look for anything you don’t recognize or didn’t install. Pay particular attention to remote-access tools such as AnyDesk, TeamViewer, UltraViewer, ConnectWise ScreenConnect, Splashtop, and similar. These are legitimate programs, but if you didn’t install them for a reason, remove them. Also look for programs installed around the time of the incident.

5. Check startup items. In Task Manager, open the Startup tab and disable anything unfamiliar.

6. Check browser extensions. In Chrome, Edge, or Firefox, open the extensions page and remove anything you don’t recognize or need. Malicious extensions can read what you type.

7. Reset the browser. Each browser has a “Reset settings” option that returns it to defaults and removes unwanted changes.

8. Check for unauthorized users. In Settings, go to Accounts, then Other users, and make sure there are no accounts you don’t recognize.

If you still see odd behavior after all of that, or if a stranger had control of your computer, move to the full wipe.

Step 1: Try a thorough scan and cleanup (Mac)

Macs are generally harder to infect, but they aren’t immune, and scams that trick you into installing something work on any computer.

1. Update macOS. Open System Settings, go to General, then Software Update.

2. Remove unknown apps. Open Finder, then Applications, and drag anything you don’t recognize to the Trash. Look especially for remote-access tools and cleaner or “optimizer” apps.

3. Check Login Items. In System Settings, open General, then Login Items, and remove anything unfamiliar.

4. Check profiles. In System Settings, search for “Profiles.” A device-management profile you didn’t install can control settings and should be removed.

5. Check browser extensions in Safari, Chrome, and Firefox, and remove ones you don’t recognize.

6. Run a trusted scanner such as Malwarebytes for Mac for a second opinion.

7. Check Users & Groups and Sharing settings. Make sure Screen Sharing, Remote Login, and Remote Management are off unless you turned them on yourself.

If a stranger controlled your Mac, go to the wipe.

Step 2: The full wipe and reinstall

This is the most thorough fix. It erases everything and reinstalls the operating system, leaving a clean computer. It takes a few hours, much of which is waiting.

Windows 10 and 11: Reset this PC

  1. Back up your personal files as described above.
  2. Open Settings, then System, then Recovery (in Windows 10, Update & Security, then Recovery).
  3. Under “Reset this PC,” choose Reset PC (or Get started).
  4. Choose Remove everything, not “Keep my files.” Keeping files is faster but can leave malware that hides inside your files or profile.
  5. Choose Cloud download if offered. It downloads a fresh copy of Windows rather than reusing the files already on the machine, which may be compromised.
  6. When asked, choose to clean the drive (sometimes listed as “Clean data” or “Remove files and clean the drive”) if you’re selling or recycling the PC, or if you want the most thorough result.
  7. Confirm and wait. The computer will restart several times.

If Windows won’t start, you can use a USB installer made on a clean computer using Microsoft’s official Media Creation Tool, and boot from it to reinstall Windows. Only use Microsoft’s official tool.

Tip: Many computers include a manufacturer recovery option. The built-in Windows reset is usually better, because manufacturer images sometimes include extra software you don’t want.

Mac: Erase All Content and Settings

On Macs from about 2018 onward with Apple silicon or the T2 security chip, and running macOS Monterey or later:

  1. Back up your personal files, ideally with Time Machine to a drive you’ll disconnect, or copy files manually.
  2. Open System Settings, then General, then Transfer or Reset, and choose Erase All Content and Settings.
  3. Follow the prompts. It signs you out of your Apple ID, turns off Find My, and erases the Mac.

On older Macs, restart while holding Command+R to enter Recovery, use Disk Utility to erase the startup disk, and then choose Reinstall macOS.

After the erase, set the Mac up as new. Don’t restore from a backup that might include the infection. Instead, copy back only your personal files.

Chromebook

Chromebooks are quite resistant to infection. If something is wrong, use Powerwash (Settings, then Reset settings, then Powerwash), which returns the device to factory state. Your files in Google Drive stay safe in your Google account.

Step 3: Rebuild the clean computer carefully

A clean wipe is only useful if you don’t reintroduce the problem.

  1. Install updates first, before doing anything else. On Windows, run Windows Update until it says you’re current. On a Mac, run Software Update.
  2. Set up a new local or standard account for daily use. Daily accounts shouldn’t have administrator rights, which limits damage if something goes wrong. Keep the administrator account for installing software.
  3. Use a strong sign-in method. On Windows, set up Windows Hello (fingerprint, face, or PIN). On Mac, use Touch ID.
  4. Turn on built-in protection. Windows Security, Firewall, and (on Windows 11) Smart App Control. On Mac, make sure FileVault disk encryption and the firewall are on.
  5. Install only what you need, from official sources. Avoid cracked software, “free” versions of paid programs, and download sites with lots of ads.
  6. Restore your personal files from the backup, and scan them first.
  7. Sign back into your accounts using new passwords, and turn on MFA. Use a password manager.
  8. Re-enable backups with an automatic solution you will actually use: OneDrive, iCloud, Google Drive, or an external drive with Windows File History or Time Machine. Keep one copy that isn’t always plugged in.
  9. Don’t reinstall remote-access tools unless you have a specific reason and trust the person you’re connecting with.

Phones: when and how to clean or reset

Phones are much harder to infect than computers, because apps are isolated from each other and from the system. When a phone is compromised, it’s usually because of one of these:

  • A malicious app installed from outside the official store, or a harmful app that slipped into the store.
  • A fake profile or configuration that gives someone control.
  • Stolen account credentials, which means the account was compromised and not the phone itself.
  • Stalkerware, an app installed by someone with access to your phone, who wants to monitor you. If this might be your situation, be careful: removing the app can alert the person. Consider contacting a domestic-violence hotline or advocate for help before changing anything, and use a safe device in the meantime.

Signs something might be wrong

Unusual battery drain, high data use, apps you didn’t install, pop-ups outside of apps, or a device that feels hot when idle. Many of these have ordinary causes too, so don’t panic over one sign.

iPhone

  1. Update iOS (Settings, General, Software Update).
  2. Review installed apps and delete anything you don’t recognize.
  3. Check for configuration profiles: Settings, General, VPN & Device Management. If a profile appears that you didn’t install, remove it.
  4. Check your Apple ID devices: Settings, tap your name, and scroll to see the list of signed-in devices. Remove any you don’t recognize.
  5. Change your Apple ID password from a clean device, and review its security settings.
  6. If problems persist, erase and set up fresh: Settings, General, Transfer or Reset iPhone, Erase All Content and Settings. Set up as new rather than restoring from a backup if you suspect the backup is affected.

Apple also offers Lockdown Mode (Settings, Privacy & Security) for people at unusually high risk. It’s strict and limits some features, so it isn’t for everyone.

Android

  1. Update Android and run Google Play Protect (in the Play Store, tap your profile, then Play Protect, then Scan).
  2. Review apps in Settings. Remove ones you don’t recognize, especially any with Device Administrator or Accessibility permissions that you didn’t grant on purpose.
  3. Check Accessibility settings. Malicious apps often abuse Accessibility to control the screen and read what’s typed. Remove any app listed there that shouldn’t be.
  4. Turn off “Install unknown apps” for every app, unless you deliberately need it.
  5. Check your Google account’s devices at myaccount.google.com, under Security, and sign out devices you don’t recognize.
  6. If problems continue, factory reset: Settings, System, Reset options, Erase all data. The exact path varies by manufacturer. Set up as new and add apps only from the Play Store.

After any phone reset

  • Install only apps from Apple’s App Store or Google Play.
  • Turn on automatic updates.
  • Use a screen lock (6-digit PIN or longer, or biometrics).
  • Review app permissions.
  • Re-enable Find My iPhone or Find My Device.
  • Call your carrier and ask for a port-out PIN or number lock.

Don’t forget the router and other devices

If someone had access to your computer, they might have seen your Wi-Fi password and, in some cases, your router settings. It’s a good idea to change the Wi-Fi password and the router’s admin password. The phone and Wi-Fi post has the steps. Also consider:

  • Tablets and other computers in the house that were on the same network or that you used to sign into the same accounts.
  • Any device you used to enter passwords during the incident.
  • Smart home devices, which should have updated firmware and unique passwords.

What about selling or donating the old computer?

A normal “delete” doesn’t remove data. Before you hand a device to someone else, do a proper factory reset, and on a computer, choose the option to clean the drive. For a computer with sensitive records, especially one that held tax or financial documents, some people prefer to physically remove the drive and keep it or have it destroyed. If a device is old and can’t get security updates anymore, consider recycling it through an official e-waste program after wiping it.

When to bring in a professional

DIY cleanup works for most home users, but it’s reasonable to get help if:

  • The computer holds business data or customer information.
  • You handle sensitive records such as health, legal, or financial data.
  • You’ve lost significant money and want a record of what happened.
  • You’re not comfortable wiping the computer yourself.
  • You suspect stalking, harassment, or targeted attacks.

If it’s a business computer, treat it as a security incident: tell whoever manages your IT, and don’t wipe it until they say so, since they may need to preserve evidence.

A printable checklist

Cleaning up after a compromise

  1. Disconnect the device from the internet if someone may be in control of it.
  2. Change key passwords from a different, clean device. Turn on MFA.
  3. Back up only personal files (no programs). Keep the backup disconnected afterward.
  4. Run a full scan plus an offline scan, then a second-opinion scan.
  5. Remove unknown programs, especially remote-access tools, and unfamiliar browser extensions.
  6. If someone had remote access, or problems persist: wipe and reinstall (Remove everything, cloud download).
  7. Update first. Use a standard account for daily use. Turn on built-in protection.
  8. Restore only scanned personal files. Don’t restore programs or system backups.
  9. Turn on automatic backups and updates.
  10. Change the Wi-Fi password and router admin password.

Frequently asked questions

Do I have to wipe my computer after a scam call? If you gave someone remote access or installed software at their request, yes, a wipe is the safest course. If you only talked to them and refused access, you don’t need to wipe, but change any passwords you shared.

Will a factory reset remove all viruses? In almost every case, yes. Very rare firmware-level infections can survive, but they are unusual for ordinary home users.

Can I keep my files during a Windows reset? You can, but if the computer was compromised I recommend “Remove everything” and restoring scanned files afterward.

Is a free antivirus enough? For most home users, the built-in Windows Security or Apple’s built-in protections plus careful habits are a solid baseline. A second-opinion scanner helps during cleanup. The biggest protection is not clicking and not installing things you weren’t expecting.

My phone had a suspicious app. Is my data stolen? It might be. Change passwords for the accounts you use on that phone, from a clean device, and turn on MFA. Watch your accounts and credit.

How long does a wipe take? Typically one to three hours for the reset, plus time to reinstall your programs and copy your files back.

What if I can’t remember my Microsoft or Apple password after the reset? Reset it from the account recovery page before you wipe, and make sure you know your recovery method. Some devices require your account credentials after a reset as an anti-theft protection.

One last thing

A clean device is a fresh start, and it can feel like a relief after the stress of being hacked. The important thing is to pair it with the habits that keep you out of trouble: unique passwords in a password manager, MFA, automatic updates, and a healthy skepticism about anyone who contacts you out of the blue.

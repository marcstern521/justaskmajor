Hacked? What to do in the first hour.

By Marc Stern · 19 min read

Sooner or later, almost everyone gets the message. A friend texts to say your Facebook account just sent them something strange. A password-reset email arrives that you never requested. Your bank sends an alert about a login from a city you’ve never visited. Or you simply try to sign in and your password no longer works.

That moment feels awful, and it’s easy to panic or freeze. The good news is that the steps to take are well known, they’re the same for almost every kind of account, and most people who act quickly get their account back with little lasting damage. The hard part is knowing the order, because doing the right things in the wrong order can leave the intruder with a way back in.

This post is written for everyone, but especially for parents, grandparents, and anyone who doesn’t think of themselves as “technical.” It has two halves. The first is what to do in the first hour if an account has been hacked, taken over, or compromised. The second is how to make it far less likely to happen again, in a way that’s realistic for real people rather than security experts.

If you’re in the middle of this right now, skip down to “The first hour” and work through it in order. Come back and read the rest once things are calm. Nobody needs to read a whole article during an emergency.

First, a few words that matter

A few terms come up constantly, and they’re less scary once they have plain names.

Hacked is the everyday word for “someone else got into my account.” It rarely involves a person in a hoodie guessing your password. Far more often, you were tricked into handing over the password or a code, or your password leaked from some other website and the criminals simply tried it elsewhere.

Phishing is a fake message, by email, text, phone call, or social media, designed to trick you into giving away a password, a code, or money. It often looks like it comes from a bank, a delivery company, Microsoft, Amazon, or a family member.

MFA (multi-factor authentication, also called two-step verification or 2FA) means signing in takes your password plus something else, such as a code from an app or a tap on your phone. It’s the single most effective protection most people can add, and we’ll spend real time on it below.

Account takeover is when someone doesn’t just peek at your account but locks you out and uses it as their own, often by changing your password and recovery information.

The first hour: what to do, in order

The steps below apply whether the account is email, Facebook, a bank, a shopping site, or anything else. Where something is specific to one kind of account, it says so.

Step 1: Stay calm, and stop using that device for anything sensitive

Take a breath. Panic is exactly what scammers count on. If you suspect the problem started with something you clicked, or with a stranger who talked their way into controlling your computer, assume the device may be affected too, not just the account.

Until you’ve done Step 2, don’t log into your bank or enter any other passwords on that computer or phone.

Step 2: Secure the device you’re using

If a stranger had remote access to your computer, for example through a “tech support” call, or you installed something you were told to install, then changing passwords on that same computer is like locking your front door while the burglar is still inside.

  • Disconnect the computer from the internet (turn off Wi-Fi or unplug the network cable) if you believe someone is controlling it right now.
  • Run a full scan with the built-in security tool: Windows Security on Windows, or your current antivirus on a Mac.
  • Make sure the operating system and your web browser are fully updated.
  • If you’re not sure the device is clean, do the next steps from a different, trusted device, such as a family member’s phone or your own phone, until you can have the computer checked.

If you gave someone remote access to your computer and also shared bank details, skip ahead to the money section below and call your bank first. Speed matters most there.

Step 3: Try to get back in, and change the password

Go to the website’s real sign-in page by typing the address yourself or using a bookmark. Don’t use a link from an email or text. Then use the “Forgot password” option.

If you can still sign in, change the password immediately. Choose a brand-new, long, unique password (more on what that means later). Do not reuse an old one, and do not use a variation of an old one.

If you can’t sign in because the intruder changed your password, don’t give up. Every major service has an account recovery process built for exactly this. The sections below cover the big ones.

Step 4: Turn on MFA, and sign out everyone else

Once you’re back in, immediately turn on two-step verification if it wasn’t on. At the same time, look for a setting called something like “Sign out of all devices,” “Where you’re logged in,” or “Security activity.” Use it to log out every other session. Otherwise the intruder may remain signed in even after you change the password.

Step 5: Check the “back doors” the intruder may have left

This is the step people most often skip, and it’s why accounts get hacked a second time. Once criminals are in, they often quietly set up ways to stay in or to keep watching. Check these:

  • Recovery email and phone number. Make sure the backup email address and phone number on the account are yours. Intruders swap them for their own so they can reset your password later.
  • Email forwarding and filters (for email accounts). Look for rules that forward your mail to another address, auto-delete messages, or move messages from your bank into a hidden folder. These are favorites for hiding fraud alerts.
  • Connected apps and devices. Look for “apps with access to your account” or “third-party access” and remove anything you don’t recognize.
  • Signature, auto-reply, and “send as” addresses. Make sure nothing has been added you didn’t set up.
  • Security questions. If the account uses them, change the answers. Better yet, use a random answer stored in your password manager. The real answer to “What was your first pet’s name?” is easily found online.

Step 6: Secure your email next, since it’s the master key

If the hacked account was anything other than your main email, check your email account right now, even if everything looks normal. Your email is the master key to almost everything else, because “Forgot password” links get sent there. Anyone with control of your email can reset your bank, social media, Amazon, and more.

If your email is where this started, fix it first, before any other account. Then work outward.

Step 7: Change passwords on other accounts, starting with the important ones

If you reused the compromised password anywhere else (many of us have), change those too. Prioritize in this order:

  1. Your main email accounts
  2. Banks, credit cards, investment, and payment apps (PayPal, Venmo, Cash App, Zelle through your bank)
  3. Your phone carrier account and Apple ID or Google account
  4. Shopping accounts with saved cards (Amazon, Walmart, and similar)
  5. Social media
  6. Everything else

If this sounds like a mountain, you’ll see in the prevention section how a password manager turns this into an afternoon of work instead of a week.

Step 8: Warn the people who may have been targeted

If your email or social media was taken over, the intruder may have messaged your contacts, often asking for money or gift cards, or sending a link that, if clicked, puts the same problem on their account. Send a short message from a different channel, such as a text or phone call, or a post from a safe account:

“My account was hacked. If you got a strange message from me, don’t click anything or send anything. I’m fixing it.”

It’s a little embarrassing and completely worth it. The people who love you would much rather get that message than send money to a scammer.

Account-specific recovery: the big ones

The companies change their screens regularly, so I’ll describe the general route and the official starting points rather than click-by-click instructions that go stale.

Facebook and Instagram

Facebook has a dedicated page for compromised accounts at facebook.com/hacked, and Instagram has a similar option on its login screen (“Forgot password?” or “Need more help?”). They can walk you through resetting the password, reviewing recent activity, and, if needed, confirming your identity, sometimes by video selfie.

Two things to know. First, be wary of anyone who offers to “recover your account” for a fee or contacts you through direct message claiming to be Facebook support. Those are scams targeting people who were just hacked. Second, if your account was used to impersonate you, you can report the impersonation, and it’s worth telling friends and family directly.

Gmail and Google accounts

Go to g.co/recover and follow the prompts. Google will ask for the last password you remember, a recovery email or phone number, or confirmation on a device you previously signed in from. Answer as many questions as you can, and try from a device and location you normally use, since that helps Google trust it’s really you.

Once back in, use Google’s Security Checkup to review devices, third-party access, and recovery details.

Microsoft accounts (Outlook, Hotmail, Microsoft 365)

Start at the Microsoft account recovery page (search for “Microsoft account recovery” and make sure the address belongs to microsoft.com). You’ll be asked for details about the account. If you’ve lost access to the recovery methods, Microsoft has a recovery form that asks for information only the real owner would know, and it can take a few days.

After you’re back in, check the Recent activity page and review sign-in history and recovery options.

Apple ID

Use iforgot.apple.com. If you have another Apple device you trust, it can often help you reset the password. Apple may also have you set up a recovery key or wait a number of days as a protection against takeover. If the process asks you to wait, wait. It’s there to protect you.

Amazon, PayPal, and shopping accounts

Use the “Forgot password” link on the real site. Then check recent orders, saved shipping addresses, saved payment methods, and any new “Buy now” or subscription settings. Call the company’s real customer service (look up the number on their website, not in an email) if anything looks wrong.

Your phone carrier

If your phone number was hijacked (“SIM swap,” where criminals move your number to a phone they control), call your carrier from another phone, ask for the fraud department, and say your account was taken over. Ask them to add a port-out PIN or number lock so your number can’t be moved without it. This matters because text-message codes from your bank and other accounts go to that number.

If money or identity is involved

If you gave out financial information, sent money, or think someone has your Social Security number, do these things quickly, in this order.

Call your bank or card company, using the number on the back of your card

Don’t use a number from an email, text, or a caller. Tell them it’s fraud. For credit cards, the law gives strong protections for unauthorized charges, and they can issue a new card. For bank accounts, report immediately, because the faster you do, the better your chances of recovering funds. Ask about freezing the account or setting up new account numbers if needed.

If you sent money by wire, gift card, or cryptocurrency

Call the bank or card issuer immediately and tell them. Report it to the FBI’s Internet Crime Complaint Center at ic3.gov. Recovery isn’t guaranteed, and I won’t pretend otherwise, but it’s much more likely when reported within hours or days than months. Report gift cards to the card issuer too.

Freeze your credit

A credit freeze stops anyone from opening new credit in your name, and it’s free by law. You do it separately with each of the three credit bureaus, Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion. A freeze doesn’t affect your existing cards or your credit score, and you can lift it temporarily when you need to apply for credit. For most people, it’s the single best identity-theft protection available.

Report identity theft and get a recovery plan

The Federal Trade Commission runs identitytheft.gov. It builds a personalized recovery plan based on what happened, helps you create an identity-theft report, and generates letters you can send. Keep copies of everything.

Other steps if your Social Security number may be exposed

  • Create your own account at ssa.gov so no one else can set one up in your name.
  • Ask the IRS about an Identity Protection PIN to prevent fraudulent tax returns in your name.
  • Check your credit reports for free at annualcreditreport.com, and review them for accounts you don’t recognize.

The one rule that covers most scams: Real banks, the IRS, Social Security, Microsoft, and Apple will not call you out of the blue and ask you to move money, buy gift cards, read them a code, or install software. If anyone does, hang up, and call the organization yourself using a number you looked up. A real emergency can survive a two-minute phone call back.

Why this happens, and what actually works to prevent it

Once things are fixed, the next question is always “How do I keep this from happening again?” Here’s the honest answer: you can’t make yourself impossible to hack, but you can make yourself a much harder target than the next person, and criminals mostly go for the easy ones.

Almost everything protective comes down to six habits. None of them requires being technical.

1. Use a password manager, and let it make long, unique passwords

The reason accounts get taken over so often is password reuse. When one website gets hacked (and it will happen to some website you use), criminals take the leaked emails and passwords and try them on banks, email, and shopping sites. If you use the same password in several places, one leak opens all of them.

A password manager is an app that creates a long random password for every site, remembers it, and fills it in for you. You only have to remember one strong main password. Options include the ones built into iPhone, Android, Chrome, and Safari, which are free and a perfectly good start, as well as dedicated apps such as Bitwarden, 1Password, and Dashlane.

For your one main password, use a passphrase, a string of four or five random, unrelated words, such as “copper-lantern-meadow-truck.” It’s long, easy to remember, and hard to crack. Length beats cleverness.

What to avoid:

  • Using the same password on more than one important site
  • Using personal details like birthdays, pet names, or street names
  • Swapping letters for symbols (“P@ssw0rd!”), which criminals’ software already tries
  • Writing passwords on sticky notes stuck to the monitor. A notebook kept at home is actually much safer than reusing a password, but a password manager is better still.

2. Turn on MFA everywhere it’s offered, starting with email

MFA means that even if a criminal gets your password, they still can’t get in. They’d also need your phone or your fingerprint. It’s the difference between a thief having your house key and a thief having your house key but not being able to get past the alarm.

Start with these accounts, in this order: your main email, your bank and investment accounts, your Apple ID or Google account, and your phone carrier, and then social media and shopping.

Not all MFA is equally strong, so here’s the ladder from best to adequate:

Method How strong Notes
Passkeys Strongest Sign in with your fingerprint, face, or phone PIN. Can’t be phished. Offered by Google, Apple, Microsoft, Amazon, and many banks.
Hardware security key Very strong A small USB or NFC key you tap. Great for high-risk people.
Authenticator app Strong Apps like Microsoft Authenticator, Google Authenticator, or your password manager show a 6-digit code that changes every 30 seconds.
Approve on your phone Good A tap to approve. Check the details before tapping “Yes” so you aren’t approving a criminal’s request.
Text message code Better than nothing Can be intercepted through SIM swapping or by trickery. Use it if it’s all that’s offered.

If you’re unsure where to begin, turn on whatever your email provider offers and use an authenticator app if possible. Anything is better than nothing.

One critical rule: never read an MFA code to someone who contacts you. A real company will never ask for your code. Scammers call or text pretending to be your bank, you receive a legitimate code as they try to log in, and then they ask you to “confirm” it. That code is the key to your account. Giving it away undoes the entire protection.

3. Learn to recognize the handful of tricks that cause most problems

Most takeovers start with a person being tricked, not with a technical break-in. The tricks change, but the patterns are consistent:

  • Urgency and fear. “Your account will be closed in 24 hours.” “Suspicious activity detected.” “A warrant has been issued.” Real organizations don’t threaten you by text.
  • A request to do something unusual, such as buy gift cards, move money to a “safe account,” read a code, or install software so someone can “fix” your computer.
  • A link or attachment you weren’t expecting, even from someone you know, whose account may have been hacked.
  • Someone you don’t know who is very friendly, especially in online dating or “investment” chats that move you toward cryptocurrency.
  • A grandchild or family member in trouble, calling or messaging urgently and asking you to keep it secret. Scammers can now copy a voice from a few seconds of audio, so a familiar voice is not proof. Hang up and call the person on their regular number.

A family code word is a wonderful low-tech defense: pick a word that everyone in the family knows and that isn’t posted anywhere, and agree to ask for it in any “emergency” request for money.

The simple habit that beats all of these is to stop, don’t click, and go to the real website or phone number yourself. Slowing down is a superpower.

4. Keep your devices and apps updated

Updates aren’t just about new features. They fix security holes that criminals actively use. Turn on automatic updates for your phone, computer, and browser, and for any apps you use regularly. Replace devices that no longer receive updates. If you have an old tablet or computer that the manufacturer has stopped supporting, it’s a risk for anything involving money or email.

5. Set up your recovery options before you need them

A surprising number of lockouts happen because the recovery email is an old address nobody checks, or the recovery phone number is a landline that was disconnected. Take ten minutes to review each important account:

  • Is the recovery email one you actively use?
  • Is the recovery phone number current?
  • Have you printed or saved the backup codes some services offer? Keep them somewhere safe at home, such as with important papers.
  • Does a trusted family member know how to find your password manager’s emergency or “legacy” access, if you want them to?

Many people also benefit from legacy contact features, such as Apple’s Legacy Contact and Google’s Inactive Account Manager, so a loved one can get access if something happens to you.

6. Make the damage smaller if something does go wrong

Assume that someday something will get through, and set things up so it costs less when it does:

  • Credit freeze at all three bureaus, as described above. Free and highly effective.
  • Alerts from your bank and credit card for transactions, logins, and new payees. Text or email, whichever you’ll actually read.
  • A separate card for online shopping, ideally a credit card, not a debit card, because credit cards carry stronger fraud protections and don’t expose your bank balance directly.
  • A separate email address for shopping and newsletters, so your main email, the one that protects everything else, stays quiet and private.
  • Regular backups of photos and documents, so a takeover or ransomware doesn’t also take your memories. A cloud backup plus a copy on an external drive is a good approach.
  • Review what’s public. Check the privacy settings on Facebook and similar sites. Scammers use details like birthdays, family names, and workplace to make their messages convincing.

A realistic plan: one weekend afternoon

If all of that sounds like too much, here’s the order I’d actually suggest to a family member. It takes a couple of hours and covers most of the benefit.

  1. Email first. Change the password to a long unique passphrase and turn on MFA (an authenticator app if you can manage it, otherwise whatever is offered). Update the recovery email and phone.
  2. Pick a password manager and install it on your phone and computer. Start saving new passwords into it.
  3. Do the money accounts next. Bank, credit cards, PayPal, Venmo. New unique passwords, MFA on, alerts on.
  4. Add MFA to your Apple ID or Google account, and to Facebook.
  5. Freeze your credit at Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion.
  6. Call your phone carrier and add a port-out PIN or number lock.
  7. Agree on a family code word.
  8. Turn on automatic updates on your phone, computer, and browser.
  9. Write down the plan, where recovery codes are kept and who to call, and tell one trusted person where it is.

You don’t have to do it all at once. Even steps 1 through 3 put you well ahead of most people.

For adult children and caregivers helping a parent

If you’re the family’s unofficial IT person, a few notes on doing this well.

Lead with respect. Being scammed has nothing to do with intelligence. Professional criminals do this full time, and they are very good at it. People who feel ashamed tend to hide what happened, and that gives criminals more time. Make it easy for your parent to tell you quickly, with no judgment.

Set it up together, not for them. Sit side by side and walk through the steps so they understand what they have. A password manager they can’t use is worse than none.

Agree on a “check with me first” rule. Anything involving money, gift cards, or “your computer has a virus” gets a call to you or another trusted person before they act. Put your number on a card by the phone.

Reduce the attack surface. Consider a freeze on their credit, call-blocking on their phone, and alerts on their bank accounts that also go to you if they’re comfortable with that.

Use the trusted-contact features. Many banks and brokerages let an account holder name a trusted contact who can be called if the firm suspects fraud or exploitation. It’s a quiet, respectful safeguard.

Keep a short written plan with the accounts that matter, how to recover them, and who to call. Hand it over in person and review it once a year.

A printable one-page card

Here is the short version. Print it, and keep it by the computer or tape it inside a kitchen cabinet.

If an account gets hacked:

  1. Stay calm. Don’t use the affected device for banking until it’s checked.
  2. Go to the real website (type it yourself). Use “Forgot password.”
  3. Choose a new, long, unique password. Turn on MFA. Sign out all other devices.
  4. Check recovery email, phone number, forwarding rules, and connected apps.
  5. Secure your email first. It’s the master key.
  6. Call your bank (number on the back of the card) if money or card numbers are involved.
  7. Freeze your credit (Equifax, Experian, TransUnion). It’s free.
  8. Report: identitytheft.gov and ic3.gov.
  9. Tell your contacts through a different channel.

Never: read a code to a caller, buy gift cards for “an emergency,” install software for a stranger, or click links in unexpected messages.

Frequently asked questions

Do I really need MFA? It’s annoying. It adds a few seconds to sign-in, and it stops the large majority of automated takeover attempts. If you only turn it on in one place, make it your email. Many services let you mark your own trusted devices so you aren’t asked every time.

Is a password manager safe? Isn’t that putting all my eggs in one basket? Reputable password managers encrypt your passwords so that even the company can’t read them, and one strong master password plus MFA protects the vault. The realistic alternative for most people is reusing a few weak passwords, which is much riskier. No tool is perfect, but this one is a large improvement for almost everyone.

What if I forget my master password? Write it down on paper and keep it somewhere safe at home, separate from the device. Some managers also offer recovery keys. Set up the recovery option when you set up the account, not afterward.

How do I know if a message is real? You usually can’t tell from the message alone, so don’t try. Look the company up yourself and contact them through the number or website you find, rather than through anything the message gave you.

Someone called claiming to be from my bank and I gave them information. What now? Hang up on any further contact. Call the real number on the back of your card and tell them what happened and what you shared. Change passwords and PINs, freeze your credit, and keep notes on what you said and when.

Should I pay someone to recover my hacked account? No. Legitimate recovery is free through the service’s own process. People who offer to “recover” accounts for a fee, especially through social media direct messages, are usually running a second scam on top of the first.

Does antivirus software keep me safe? It helps with malicious software, but most takeovers happen because of tricked people and reused passwords, which antivirus can’t fix. Built-in protections such as Windows Security and Apple’s built-in defenses are a solid baseline for most home users. Think of antivirus as one layer, not the whole defense.

Can I get my money back? Sometimes. Credit card fraud is usually reversible. Bank transfers are harder, and wire transfers, gift cards, and crypto are the hardest to recover. The single biggest factor is how quickly you report, so don’t wait until you’re sure.

One last thing

If you take away only three things from all of this, make them these: put MFA on your email, use a different password for every important account (let a password manager do the remembering), and when anything feels urgent or strange, stop and go look up the real phone number yourself.

Those three habits stop the great majority of the account takeovers I see. They don’t require being technical, and they cost little or nothing.

If you’d like help setting any of this up, for yourself, a parent, or a small business, that’s what I do. You can reach me through the contact page. And if you’re reading this because it already happened, I’m sorry. It’s more common than people admit, and it’s fixable. Start with the first hour, take it one step at a time, and you’ll get through it.

What AI actually changes for a 10-person business.

By Marc Stern · 15 min read

Somewhere in your office right now, someone is using an AI tool. You may not know which one, and you almost certainly didn’t approve it.

Maybe it’s the bookkeeper pasting a client’s spreadsheet into a free chatbot to “clean it up.” Maybe it’s a salesperson uploading a customer list to get a better email draft. Maybe it’s a manager feeding a draft employee review into a website they found last week. Nobody is being reckless. They’re doing what people always do with a useful tool: they’re using it, and they’re asking forgiveness instead of permission.

That’s the real AI question for a small business in 2026. It isn’t “will AI take over my company?” or “should I buy something with AI in the name?” It’s much more practical: how do I get the benefit of these tools without leaking customer data, creating legal exposure, or wasting money on hype?

I’ve spent my career in environments where a bad configuration decision can cost real money, and the discipline that applies there applies here too, at a smaller scale. This post is the version I’d give an owner over coffee. Five things worth doing, three things worth ignoring, and a one-page policy you can steal.

The short version: Pick one approved AI tool on a business account, tell people what data stays out of it, fix your file permissions before you give an AI assistant access to your files, and measure whether it saves time. Skip the custom “AI agents,” the AI-detector software, and the vendor pitches promising to transform your business.

What AI is actually good at (and not)

It helps to be honest about the tools before you build rules around them. Today’s AI assistants are very good at a particular set of jobs and unreliable at others.

Good at:

  • First drafts of emails, proposals, job postings, policies, and marketing copy
  • Summarizing long documents, email threads, and meeting transcripts
  • Rewording things for a different audience or tone
  • Explaining spreadsheets, formulas, and unfamiliar technical terms
  • Brainstorming options and checking your own work for gaps
  • Turning messy notes into organized outlines

Not reliable at:

  • Facts it hasn’t been given. These tools can state wrong things with total confidence, including invented citations, prices, and legal rules.
  • Math on large data sets, unless connected to a proper spreadsheet or code tool.
  • Anything where being wrong is expensive and nobody will check: legal wording, tax positions, medical or safety guidance, contract terms.
  • Knowing your business. It doesn’t know your customers, your history, or your standards unless you tell it.

The mental model I use: an AI assistant is a fast, tireless intern who has read a lot, never says “I don’t know,” and has never worked at your company. You’d happily let that intern draft something. You would not let them send it to a client unsupervised.

The data problem nobody mentions in the sales pitch

Most of the risk in small-business AI use isn’t the AI doing something strange. It’s where your information goes when someone pastes it in.

When you use a consumer or free version of a tool, the terms of service often allow the provider to retain your conversations, and in some cases use them to improve their models. Business and enterprise versions typically offer stronger commitments: your data isn’t used to train public models, there are admin controls, and sometimes audit logs. The exact terms differ by vendor and plan, and they change, so read the current terms for whatever you use rather than assuming.

The practical consequence is simple. A free personal account is the wrong place for client data, and your employees are probably using one anyway because it’s the one they already have.

Here are examples of what’s at stake:

  • A medical or dental office pasting patient information into a chatbot may be creating a HIPAA problem.
  • A law or accounting firm pasting client financials may be breaching confidentiality duties.
  • Anyone pasting passwords, API keys, or banking details is handing over exactly what an attacker wants.
  • A nonprofit pasting donor lists is exposing people who trusted you with their information.

This isn’t a reason to ban AI. Banning it doesn’t work: people use it on their phones and personal laptops. It’s a reason to give people a safe, approved way to do it.

Five things worth doing

1. Pick one approved tool, on a business account

Choose one assistant, put everyone on the business-tier version with a work login, and say so out loud. Having one sanctioned option does more than any rule, because people stop reaching for their personal accounts when the official tool is good enough.

If you already run Microsoft 365, the obvious place to start is Microsoft Copilot Chat, which you sign in to with your work account. Microsoft has been bundling Copilot Chat capabilities into the Business plans, and a paid add-on, Microsoft 365 Copilot Business, goes further by working inside Word, Excel, Outlook, and Teams using your own files and email. I’ve seen it listed at about $21 per user per month on an annual commitment, but confirm the price and eligibility with Microsoft before you plan around it. Other business-tier options from Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google exist as well, and they have similar controls.

How to choose: ask which tool fits where your people already work, which one gives you admin controls and a clear data-handling commitment, and which one your team will actually use. Don’t pick based on a benchmark. Pick based on fit.

Then buy a small number of licenses, test for a month, and expand only if people use it. Paying for forty seats nobody opens is the classic mistake.

2. Write a one-page AI use policy

You don’t need a legal treatise. You need one page, in plain language, that every employee reads and acknowledges. Here’s a template you can adapt:

Acceptable Use of AI Tools — [Company Name]

Approved tools: [Tool name] using your company account. Don’t use personal AI accounts for company work.

Never enter: client or patient personal information, financial account numbers, passwords or access keys, social security numbers, unreleased financial results, or anything covered by a confidentiality agreement.

Always review: AI output is a draft. A named person is responsible for checking facts, figures, names, and tone before anything goes to a client, a regulator, or the public.

Disclose when it matters: if a client or contract requires you to disclose AI use, you do.

When in doubt, ask: [Name] at [email] can answer questions and approve new tools.

Report mistakes: if you pasted something you shouldn’t have, tell [Name] right away. You won’t be in trouble for reporting it. You will be if you hide it.

The last line is the most important. People who fear punishment hide mistakes, and hidden mistakes are the expensive ones.

3. Sort your data into three buckets

Staff don’t want to read a long rulebook. They want to know, in two seconds, whether something is okay to paste. A three-color system works:

Color What it is Rule
Green Public or harmless: your own marketing copy, published web content, generic questions Fine to use in any approved tool
Yellow Internal but not sensitive: draft policies, internal process notes, non-identifying business plans Approved business tool only
Red Regulated or confidential: client and patient data, financials, HR records, credentials Don’t paste, unless a specific tool has been approved for that data

Print it and tape it up if you have to. It works because it replaces judgment calls with a quick sort.

4. Fix your permissions before you turn on AI that can search your files

This is the one I’d emphasize most, because it’s the least obvious and the most likely to bite.

Tools like Microsoft 365 Copilot don’t add new access. They work on whatever the signed-in user can already reach. That sounds safe, and it is, if your permissions are actually right. But in many small tenants, they aren’t. A folder containing payroll, someone’s performance reviews, or the owner’s personal finances gets shared “with everyone” years ago and forgotten. Nobody ever stumbled on it because nobody searched for it. An AI assistant will happily find it the first time an employee asks, “What does everyone earn here?”

The tool didn’t create the exposure. It made an old, invisible mistake easy to trigger.

Before rolling out any assistant that reads your files and email:

  1. Review who has access to your sensitive libraries. Start with HR, finance, and executive folders in SharePoint and OneDrive.
  2. Find “Anyone with the link” shares and remove the ones you don’t recognize.
  3. Remove “Everyone” and “Everyone except external users” from sensitive sites.
  4. Make sure departed employees are really gone and their files have owners.
  5. Re-test by signing in as a regular employee and trying to find something you shouldn’t be able to.

I cover the settings that control this in Microsoft 365 Security Basics, in plain English. It’s also a good project on its own, with or without AI.

5. Start with three small use cases and measure them

Resist the urge to “transform the business.” Pick three boring, repetitive tasks, run them for a month, and see what changes. Good candidates for a small business:

  • Email and proposal drafting. The assistant produces a first pass from a few bullet points. The person edits it. Measure minutes saved per message.
  • Meeting notes and follow-ups. Record or transcribe a meeting (with consent), summarize it, and list action items. Measure whether follow-ups actually happen faster.
  • Spreadsheet and data help. “Explain what this formula does.” “Write a formula to count rows where column B is blank.” “Summarize the trends in this export.” Measure how often people get unstuck without asking someone.

For each one, write down the baseline before you start: how long it takes now, and who does it. After a month, ask two questions: Did it save real time? Was the quality acceptable after review? If both answers are yes, expand. If not, drop it without guilt. That’s what a pilot is for.

A sample of what honest measurement looks like:

Task Before After (with review) Verdict
Weekly client update email 25 min 10 min Keep
Summarizing vendor contracts 40 min 30 min, plus extra legal review Not worth it
Meeting action items 15 min 3 min Keep

Notice that one of those failed. That’s the point. You want to learn which uses work before you’ve built your workflow around them.

Three things worth ignoring

1. Custom “AI agents” and automation platforms, for now

There’s a lot of excitement about autonomous agents that read your inbox, update your CRM, and take actions on their own. For a ten-person company, that’s almost always premature. Industry analysts have warned that a large share of agentic AI projects will be canceled because of cost, unclear value, and weak controls. Agents that can act need much more careful permissions, testing, and monitoring than an assistant that only suggests.

Get comfortable with assistants that draft and summarize first. If you later want automation, start with something small and reversible, and make sure there’s a human approval step before anything is sent, paid, or deleted.

2. AI-detection software and blanket bans

Some owners respond to AI by buying tools that claim to detect AI-written text, or by banning it outright. Neither works well. Detectors are unreliable, and they produce false accusations against people who wrote their own work. Bans push use underground, onto personal phones, where you have no visibility and no controls.

A better approach is the policy above: approved tools, clear data rules, and review of anything that goes out the door. Judge the work on whether it’s accurate and good, not on how it was produced.

3. Every “AI-powered” vendor pitch

Within the next year, nearly every software vendor you use will add an AI feature, and some will charge extra for it. Some of these features are real improvements. Many are a chatbot bolted on to justify a price increase.

Before you pay for an AI add-on, ask the vendor these questions:

  1. What specific task does this save time on, and by how much?
  2. Where does my data go, and is it used to train your models?
  3. Can I turn it off, and can I see an audit log of what it did?
  4. What happens to my data if I cancel?
  5. Is the AI feature priced separately, and can I trial it first?

If the answers are vague, you have your answer.

What’s okay and what isn’t: ten everyday scenarios

Rules are easier to follow when you can see them applied. Here’s how the green, yellow, and red buckets work out for situations that come up in a typical office. Use these in your training, and add your own as questions arise.

Scenario Verdict Why
Ask the assistant to rewrite a paragraph of your own website copy Green Public content, nothing sensitive.
Paste an email from a customer and ask for a polite reply Yellow Fine in the approved business tool. Remove account numbers or personal details first.
Upload a spreadsheet of customer names, addresses, and balances for “cleanup” Red Personal and financial data. Use an approved tool built for that data, or clean it by hand or with a script.
Summarize a vendor contract Yellow Approved tool only. Have a person (or your attorney) verify anything that affects money or liability.
Draft a job posting Green No sensitive data involved. Check it for accuracy and for legal wording.
Paste a draft performance review and ask for “better wording” Red HR record about an identifiable person. Write it yourself or use a tool approved for HR.
Ask how to write an Excel formula, using fake sample data Green The technique is the question, not your data.
Paste an error message or log from your software Yellow Often harmless, but check it doesn’t contain passwords, keys, or customer data.
Record a client meeting and generate notes Yellow to red Get consent from everyone on the call. Some clients and some states require it. Check client contracts.
Ask an assistant for tax or legal advice and act on it directly Red Use it to prepare questions for your accountant or attorney, not to replace them.

Notice how many entries come down to the same two habits: remove what’s sensitive before you paste, and verify what comes back before you act.

Contracts, insurance, and regulated data

Three areas catch small businesses by surprise, because the rules aren’t about AI at all. They’re about data you were already responsible for.

Client contracts. Many master service agreements and NDAs restrict how you handle the other party’s information. Some now include explicit language about AI tools. Before you paste anything from a client into any third-party service, check whether your contract allows it. If it’s unclear, ask the client. Most will appreciate that you did.

Cyber insurance. Insurers are increasingly adding AI questions to their applications, alongside the usual ones about MFA and backups. Answer honestly. A claim can be contested when what you said on the application doesn’t match what you were actually doing.

Regulated data. If you handle health information, financial records, children’s data, or payment card data, you may have specific legal duties that don’t go away because a tool is convenient. Healthcare organizations, for instance, generally need a signed business associate agreement with any vendor that touches protected health information. Ask the vendor whether they offer one for the tool and plan you’re considering, and don’t assume the answer. When in doubt, this is a question for your compliance advisor or attorney, and I’m not either of those.

Questions I hear from owners

“Can’t I just tell everyone not to use it?” You can, but it won’t hold. People will use it on their phones, and now you have no visibility and no controls. An approved option plus clear rules works better than a ban.

“Will it replace my employees?” In my experience, for a small business, the realistic near-term effect is that it removes tedious work from people’s days, such as first drafts, summaries, and formatting. It doesn’t replace judgment, relationships, or accountability. The risk isn’t that AI takes someone’s job. It’s that a competitor of yours gets faster at the routine parts while you’re still doing them by hand.

“What if it makes something up?” It will, sometimes. That’s why the policy says a named person reviews everything before it goes out. Treat anything with a number, a name, a quote, or a citation as unverified until you’ve checked it against a real source.

“Is it worth paying for?” Often, but only for the people who use it daily. A free or already-included tool is fine for occasional use. Pay for seats that actually save someone an hour a week or more, which at typical wages covers a license cost quickly. Don’t pay for seats that don’t.

“Should we tell customers we use AI?” Where a contract, regulation, or industry norm requires it, yes. Otherwise, use judgment: if the AI wrote something a client would reasonably expect a person to have written and personally stand behind, a person should have actually reviewed and stood behind it.

“What do we do about meeting recorders and transcription bots that join calls on their own?” Treat these like any other tool: approved ones only. Unapproved note-taking bots joining client calls, and storing transcripts on a vendor’s servers, are one of the more common ways confidential conversations leak. Decide on a policy and let clients know about it up front.

The risk that’s growing fastest: AI-assisted fraud

While you’re thinking about how you use AI, remember that criminals are using it too. The FBI’s 2025 Internet Crime Report counted more than 22,000 complaints with an AI connection, with adjusted losses above $893 million, and noted this is likely an undercount because victims often can’t tell whether AI was involved.

For a small business, this shows up in a few ways:

  • Better phishing emails. The awkward grammar that used to give scams away is gone.
  • Voice cloning. A caller who sounds exactly like your owner or your bank rep asks for an urgent transfer.
  • Fake invoices and vendor changes that match your real vendors’ formatting.

The defense is the same boring procedural one: verify any unusual money request through a second channel, using contact information you already had. If the “owner” calls asking for a wire, hang up and call back on their known number. Agree on a code word with your key people for emergencies if that helps. A process beats a gut feeling, because the whole point of these attacks is to defeat your gut feeling.

A realistic 30-day rollout

Week What to do
1 Inventory what people already use (ask, don’t police). Choose one approved tool. Review permissions on HR, finance, and executive folders.
2 Write and share the one-page policy and the green/yellow/red chart. Buy a handful of licenses. Pick three pilot use cases and record baselines.
3 Run the pilot with five to ten volunteers. Hold a 20-minute “show and tell” so people share what works.
4 Review the numbers. Keep what saved time, drop what didn’t. Decide whether to expand, and add the callback rule for money requests if you haven’t already.

None of this requires a big budget. It requires about a day of your time spread over a month, plus whatever you decide to spend on licenses.

Prompts that work (and a habit that helps)

If you’re helping staff get started, a few patterns make a bigger difference than any tool choice:

  • Give context. “Write a polite follow-up to a customer who hasn’t paid a 45-day-old invoice” gets better results than “write a follow-up email.”
  • Say who it’s for and what tone you want. “Friendly but firm, under 120 words, for a long-time customer.”
  • Paste your own examples. “Here are two emails I wrote that I like. Match this style.”
  • Ask it to check itself. “List any claims in this draft that I should verify before sending.”
  • Iterate. The first response is a start. Reply with “shorter,” “less formal,” or “add a deadline.”

And the one habit that matters most: read it before you send it. Every embarrassing AI story, from fake legal citations to wrong prices in customer emails, comes down to someone skipping that step.

Quick checklist

Where I’d start

If I were running a ten-person company tomorrow, I’d do two things this week. First, fix the permissions problem, because it’s valuable regardless of AI and it’s the one that can hurt you. Second, write the one-page policy and pick the tool. Everything else can wait until you’ve seen whether people actually use what you’ve provided.

AI is a genuinely useful tool, and the businesses that benefit most will be the ones that treat it like any other tool: chosen on purpose, configured carefully, and watched with a little skepticism.

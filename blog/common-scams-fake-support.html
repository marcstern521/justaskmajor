Common scams and fake support calls.

By Marc Stern · 14 min read

If you’ve ever picked up the phone and heard “this is the fraud department at your bank” or watched a pop-up take over your screen and tell you that your computer is infected, you’ve met the scams in this post. Nearly everyone has, and the people who run them are good at it. They do it full time, they run it like a business, and they test their scripts the way a marketing team tests an ad.

That’s the first thing worth saying clearly: falling for a scam says nothing about your intelligence. It says the scammer picked a good moment. Scams work by creating fear, urgency, or trust at exactly the moment you can’t think it through. I’ve seen smart, careful people, including IT professionals, nearly fall for a well-timed text. The defense isn’t being smarter. It’s having a few simple rules decided in advance, so you don’t have to make good decisions under pressure.

This is the companion to Hacked? What to Do in the First Hour. That post covers what to do after an account is compromised. This one is about spotting the trick before it works, with special attention to the “I’m from support” scams that target so many people, and especially older adults.

Five rules that beat almost every scam in this post:

  1. Real companies and agencies don’t call, text, or email you out of the blue to ask for money, codes, passwords, or remote access to your device.
  2. If someone creates urgency or fear, that’s the warning sign, not a reason to hurry.
  3. Never move money, buy gift cards, or send crypto because a caller or a message told you to.
  4. Never read a verification code to anyone who contacted you.
  5. When in doubt, hang up and call the real number yourself, from your own contacts, the back of your card, or the company’s website.

How scams actually work (the pattern behind all of them)

Scams look different on the surface, but nearly all of them follow the same four steps, and knowing the steps makes them easier to spot.

Step one is contact. It might be a phone call, a text, an email, a pop-up, a social media message, or a letter. The scammer needs to reach you, and they cast a wide net. Many “targeted” messages are really sent to millions of people.

Step two is a story. The story gives you a reason to act: your account was compromised, you owe money, you’ve won something, a loved one is in trouble, your computer has a virus, a package couldn’t be delivered. Good stories contain a little truth. They use real company names, real logos, and sometimes real details pulled from data breaches.

Step three is pressure. Time limits, threats, secrecy, and authority all push you toward acting before you check. “Don’t tell anyone, the bank employees are in on it” is a classic line, and it’s designed to cut you off from the people who could talk you out of it.

Step four is the ask. Money by wire transfer, gift cards, cryptocurrency, or a payment app. Or access: a password, a verification code, or control of your computer. Once you’ve done it, it’s usually hard or impossible to undo.

If you remember nothing else, remember that the way out is almost always at step three: slow down and verify through a channel you control.

The “support” scams

These are the ones most people ask me about, so they get the most space.

Fake tech support: the pop-up and the phone call

How it works. A loud, alarming page pops up on your screen, sometimes with a sound or a voice. It says your computer is infected, your account is locked, or your Microsoft or Apple license has expired, and it shows a phone number to call. Or you get a call from someone who says they’re from Microsoft, Apple, your internet provider, or “Windows support” and that they’ve detected a problem.

When you call, or when they call you, a friendly technician walks you through “fixing” it. Usually that means installing a remote-access program so they can “see” your screen. Once they have access, they may show you scary-looking but harmless system messages, “find” fake infections, charge you hundreds of dollars for a pointless cleanup, steal your passwords, or log into your bank while you watch.

Why it works. The screen looks official, the technician sounds professional and patient, and the program they ask you to install feels like a normal part of getting help.

The truth.

  • Microsoft, Apple, and Google do not show you pop-ups with a phone number to call, and they do not call you to report a problem with your computer.
  • A real warning from your own security software doesn’t ask you to phone anyone.
  • No legitimate company will ask you to install remote-control software unless you contacted them first, through a number you looked up yourself.

What to do about a pop-up. Don’t call the number. Don’t click anything in the window, including the “X” or “Cancel,” which can trigger more. Instead, close the browser the hard way: on Windows, press Ctrl+Alt+Delete, open Task Manager, select the browser, and choose End task. On a Mac, press Option+Command+Esc and Force Quit the browser. When you reopen it, don’t restore the previous tabs. If a page keeps coming back, restart the computer.

What to do if you already called or gave access. Turn off the computer’s internet immediately (disconnect Wi-Fi or unplug the cable) and shut down the remote-access program if you can find it. Then follow the steps in the recovery post, and see how to clean or wipe a computer. If you paid, call your bank or card company right away and tell them what happened. If you paid with a card, say so in the first sentence, because card payments have the strongest protections. Report it at reportfraud.ftc.gov.

The “bank fraud department” call

How it works. The call (or text) says there’s suspicious activity on your account, and the caller wants to help you protect your money. They may know your name and even part of your card number. The caller ID may genuinely show your bank’s name, because caller ID is easy to fake. They ask you to confirm a code sent to your phone, which is actually a login code for your account, or tell you to move your money to a “safe account” while they investigate.

The truth. Your bank will never ask you to move your money to protect it. There is no such thing as a safe account the bank tells you to transfer to. And no real bank employee needs to know the one-time code sent to your phone.

What to do. Hang up. Call the number on the back of your card. If the call was real, the bank will see the alert and can help you. If it wasn’t, you’ve avoided the scam.

A related version targets people who use payment apps like Zelle, Venmo, or Cash App: the caller says someone is trying to take money from your account, and you must send money to yourself, to a “fraud” account, or to a phone number to reverse it. Those payments are usually impossible to recall. Treat any call that ends in “send money” as a scam.

Government and agency impersonators

How it works. A caller claims to be from the IRS, the Social Security Administration, Medicare, or law enforcement. They say you owe money, a warrant has been issued, your Social Security number has been “suspended,” or your benefits will stop. They demand payment by gift card, wire, or cryptocurrency, often with threats of arrest.

The truth. Government agencies don’t call to demand immediate payment, and they never demand payment by gift card, cryptocurrency, or wire transfer. Your Social Security number can’t be suspended. Real agencies generally contact you first by mail. The IRS and the Social Security Administration don’t threaten arrest over the phone. And Medicare doesn’t call to sell you something or “verify” your number to send a new card.

What to do. Hang up. If you’re worried it might be real, look up the agency’s number yourself and call. If it’s Medicare, 1-800-MEDICARE is the real line.

The “refund” scam

How it works. Someone claims they accidentally refunded too much, or that you’re owed a refund for a subscription or a technical-support service you “canceled.” They ask to connect to your computer to process it. Once connected, they make it look as if they deposited too much into your account, and ask you to send back the “extra.” The money they appear to have sent was just a transfer from your own savings account to your checking.

What to do. Don’t allow remote access. Don’t send money back. Check your bank account directly, through your bank’s real app or website, and call the bank.

Utility and “service shutoff” calls

How it works. A caller says your power, water, or internet will be cut off in an hour unless you pay now, usually by gift card or payment app. Sometimes they claim to be from a company you really do use.

What to do. Hang up and call the number on your actual bill. Real utilities send written notices and give you time. They don’t demand gift cards.

Fake customer-service numbers

How it works. You search online for “Amazon customer service” or “Delta Airlines phone number” and click one of the top results. Some of those results are ads or fake pages that show a scammer’s phone number. The person who answers sounds helpful, then asks for your account details, card number, or remote access.

What to do. Go straight to the company’s website by typing the address, or use the phone number on a statement or the back of your card. Be extra careful with phone numbers that appear in search ads, social media posts, or pop-ups.

Scams that play on love, worry, and trust

The grandchild and family emergency

How it works. A call or message says, “Grandma, it’s me. I’m in trouble,” with a story about a car accident, an arrest, or being stranded abroad, and a plea to send money and not tell the parents. Scammers can now copy a person’s voice from a few seconds of audio taken from social media, so the voice may sound right.

What to do. Hang up and call the person on their regular number, or call another family member. Use your family code word, which is a word only your family knows, never posted online, that anyone in a real emergency can supply. If the caller says to keep it secret, that’s the biggest warning of all.

Romance and “friendship” scams

How it works. Someone befriends you on social media or a dating site, builds a relationship over weeks or months, and eventually has an emergency or an opportunity that requires money. They may have never met you in person and always have a reason why they can’t video chat.

Warning signs. Fast declarations of love, refusing to meet or appear on live video, a story that involves working overseas, military service, or an oil rig, and eventually a request for money or help with a “transfer.”

What to do. Never send money to someone you haven’t met in person. A reverse image search of their photos can reveal that they’re stolen. Talk to someone you trust before sending anything.

Investment and cryptocurrency scams

How it works. A friendly stranger, a text message that seems to have been sent to the wrong number, or an ad promises big returns, often tied to cryptocurrency. You’re shown a slick dashboard with growing profits. When you try to withdraw, you’re told you must pay fees or taxes first. Some scammers insist on a Bitcoin ATM.

The truth. Guaranteed high returns don’t exist. Legitimate businesses don’t ask you to pay by Bitcoin ATM or gift card. Real government and bank staff never send you to a Bitcoin machine.

Charity and disaster scams

After a hurricane, flood, or wildfire, fake charities appear. Check a charity’s name through a charity-rating site before donating, and give through the organization’s official website, not through a link or a caller.

Scams that arrive by text, email, and QR code

The package and toll texts

How it works. You get a text saying a package couldn’t be delivered, or that you owe an unpaid toll or parking fee, with a link to “fix” it. The page looks real and asks for a small payment, which steals your card details.

What to do. Don’t tap the link. If you’re expecting a package, check directly with the retailer or carrier through their official app or website. Forward unwanted texts to 7726 (SPAM), then delete them.

Phishing emails

Warning signs. Urgent threats, unexpected attachments, a sender address that doesn’t quite match the company, generic greetings like “Dear customer,” and links whose real address (visible when you hover over them) doesn’t match the company. Even good-looking emails can be fake, so the safest habit is never to log in through an email link. Go to the site directly.

QR code scams

QR codes can lead anywhere, and scammers put fake stickers over real ones on parking meters, restaurant tables, and posters. Treat QR codes like any other link: be careful about paying or logging in after scanning one that was stuck on in public, and check that the web address that opens matches the business you expected.

The “wrong number” text

A message like “Hi, are we still on for dinner?” from an unknown number, followed by a friendly chat, is often the first step toward a romance or investment scam. Don’t reply. Block and delete.

Fake job offers and marketplace scams

Fake employers ask you to pay for equipment, deposit a check and send some back, or provide your Social Security number early. No real employer asks you to send money from a check they gave you. On online marketplaces, be wary of buyers who overpay and ask for a refund, and of anyone who wants you to use a payment method with no protection.

The warning signs, in one list

If any of these appear, stop and verify.

  • You didn’t initiate the contact.
  • Urgency or fear: “right now,” “within the hour,” “you’ll be arrested.”
  • Secrecy: “don’t tell your bank, your family, or the police.”
  • Unusual payment: gift cards, wire transfers, cryptocurrency, payment apps, Bitcoin ATMs, or cash in an envelope.
  • A request for a code, a password, or access to your computer.
  • Too good to be true: prizes you didn’t enter for, guaranteed returns, “free” money.
  • Pressure to stay on the phone while you go to the bank or buy gift cards.

What to do in the moment

Having a script ready makes it easier. Here is one you can say on any suspicious call:

“I’m going to hang up and call the number on my card. If this is real, I’ll hear from you again.”

Then hang up. Don’t argue, don’t explain, and don’t worry about being rude. A genuine caller won’t mind. A scammer will try to keep you on the line or get angry, which tells you what you need to know.

Some practical protections:

  • Let unknown calls go to voicemail. Real callers leave messages.
  • Turn on your phone’s spam-call filtering, and ask your carrier about call-blocking tools.
  • Register on the National Do Not Call Registry (donotcall.gov). It won’t stop scammers, but it reduces legitimate telemarketing so the remaining calls stand out.
  • Put your credit on freeze so a stolen identity can’t be used to open accounts. It’s free.
  • Set bank alerts so you see unusual activity quickly.
  • Agree on a family code word, and say it out loud with your family this week.
  • Keep a “check with me first” rule for any request involving money or computer access, and call a trusted person before acting.

If you’ve been scammed

First, you’re not alone, and acting quickly matters more than feeling embarrassed.

  1. Stop all contact with the scammer. Don’t send any more money, even to “recover” what was lost.
  2. Call your bank or card issuer right away (use the number on the card). Tell them it’s fraud. The faster you report, the better the chance of stopping or reversing a transaction.
  3. If you gave remote access, disconnect the computer from the internet and follow the clean-up guide. Change passwords from a different, clean device.
  4. Freeze your credit at Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion.
  5. Report it: reportfraud.ftc.gov, ic3.gov for internet crime, and identitytheft.gov if your identity was used. Report text scams to 7726.
  6. Tell someone you trust. The shame is what scammers rely on to keep you quiet.
  7. Watch out for recovery scams. After you’re scammed, you may be contacted by someone offering to get your money back for a fee. That’s another scam.

For families: helping a parent or grandparent

If you’re helping an older family member, a few things work well.

Talk about scams before they happen, the way you’d talk about locking the car. Share the five rules. Tell a story or two so it feels real.

Make it safe to confess. The most dangerous scam outcome is the one hidden out of embarrassment. Say out loud: “If anything strange ever happens, call me. I won’t be upset.”

Reduce the ways in. Call-blocking, a credit freeze, bank alerts (some banks let you set up a trusted contact), and a rule that any unusual request gets a call to you first.

Practice the script. Role-play a “bank” call once. It sounds silly, but a rehearsed response is much easier to use under stress.

A quick reference card

Print or screenshot this.

If someone calls, texts, or pops up saying you have a problem:

  • Hang up or close the window. Don’t call the number shown.
  • Look up the real number yourself and call.
  • Never share a code, a password, or remote access.
  • Never pay with gift cards, crypto, wire, or payment apps because someone told you to.
  • Tell someone you trust.

If you’ve already acted: call your bank, disconnect the device from the internet, change passwords from a clean device, freeze your credit, and report at reportfraud.ftc.gov.

Frequently asked questions

Why do scammers know my name and address? That information is often available from public records, data brokers, and past data breaches. Knowing your details doesn’t mean the caller is legitimate.

The caller ID shows my bank. Doesn’t that prove it’s them? No. Caller ID can be faked, a practice called spoofing. Hang up and call back using a number you trust.

What if I think it’s real but I’m not sure? Verify independently. Real emergencies survive a two-minute callback.

Should I answer unknown calls just to see? It’s fine to let them go to voicemail. Answering confirms your number is active and may lead to more calls.

Is it safe to click “unsubscribe” on spam? For legitimate companies, yes. For obvious spam, no. Clicking can confirm your address is real. Just delete it or mark it as spam.

Can scammers really clone a voice? Yes, with just a short audio clip, which is why a family code word is so valuable.

Can I get my money back? Sometimes, especially with credit cards and when you report quickly. Wires, gift cards, and crypto are the hardest to recover. Report immediately and never pay a “recovery” service.

One last thing

Scammers are betting that you’ll act before you think. The best thing you can do is take away their advantage by pausing. Hang up, verify, ask someone. Slowing down is not rude, and it’s not paranoid. It’s the one move that nearly every scam can’t survive.
